A commenting unit only earns its place on a page if it pulls people in. This one had grown stale: a couple of basic counts and a generic emoji tray that did nothing for the brand. The redesign turned it into a reason to participate, and to sign up.

## Reading the old unit

The original let people post and read comments, but it surfaced almost nothing about the conversation. Comment count and active users, and not much else. The reactions were off-the-shelf emoji that carried no meaning back to the publisher.

## An overview worth watching

The new overview gives the thread a pulse. It shows total comments, how many people are active on the page, whether the thread is trending within a configurable window, thread views, and a single total-reactions number that rolls up upvotes, downvotes, replies, and emoji into one signal.

The layout went through several rounds as requirements firmed up, landing on something compact and editorial. A Top Comment surfaces the highest-engagement reply, ranked by replies and refreshed hourly, with show-more to expand. Top Terms lists the five most-used words so readers can filter the thread by what people are actually talking about.

## Reactions that mean something

The biggest call was cutting a library of over a hundred emoji down to a focused set of emotion and vote options. Fewer, clearer choices did two things at once: they reduced the toxic edge that a giant emoji wall invites, and they produced clean, comparable data the publisher could actually benchmark.

## Outcome

The redesign gave the unit something to differentiate on, standardised how engagement gets measured, trimmed the features no one used, and pointed the whole thing at one goal: turning a passing reader into a registered user, then handing engineering a clean component library to build it.
