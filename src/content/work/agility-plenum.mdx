Agility CMS had grown fast. The interface had been pushed forward through prototyping, ahead of any real UX research, which is normal for a product at that stage. My job was to bring a mature design process into the product without slowing it down, and to leave behind something the whole team could build on.

## The challenge

The product looked the part but had never been examined as a user journey. Decisions were made for the screen in front of you, not for the path through the tool. That worked while the CMS was young. It stopped working as it grew, because there was no shared reference for how anything should look or behave.

So I framed the work around two outcomes: understand how people actually use the CMS, and give design and engineering one source of truth.

## My role

As product designer I steered the redesign from concept through post-launch refinement. In practice that meant:

- Aligning goals across design, development, and customer success
- Building Plenum, a design system, as the shared framework
- Running surveys, interviews, and usability tests at each stage
- Holding Pixel Perfection workshops with engineers to protect design intent
- Keeping user needs ahead of aesthetics in every call

## A real feedback loop

I paired qualitative and quantitative research so neither could mislead us. Interviews and usability tests gave direct signal. Surveys and analytics showed the broader pattern. Together they pointed at the work that mattered, and the early concepts started from there.

The first round of changes was concrete, not cosmetic:

- **Home dashboard** simplified around the tools people actually open first
- **Content management** reworked to cut steps out of everyday tasks
- **Digital assets** restructured for clearer filing and faster retrieval
- **Flexible layouts** that hand control of the page back to the user
- **Advanced search** with filters that make content findable

## Plenum, the design system

Plenum gave the product one visual and behavioural language. I set its principles first — simplicity, scalability, usability — then built reusable components against them and wrote the guidelines for putting them together. Storybook held the live components, so engineers could develop and test each one in isolation and reuse it everywhere.

## Pages became Layouts

The biggest single change was a word. "Pages" became "Layouts", because that is how people actually think about structuring a site. Renaming it forced a rethink of the interaction model underneath. Drag and drop let users see and shape the content hierarchy directly, in line with the real architecture of their site.

## Across the product

The same thinking carried through Digital Assets, Models, Reports, and Settings. Models gained a visual, drag-to-reorganise view of content hierarchies. Reports pulled recent changes, ready-to-publish items, batches, and activity into one place. Settings was overhauled so global configuration, locales, and access control were reachable without losing any existing power.

## Outcome

What started as a UI refresh turned into something more useful: a working relationship between the product and its users. External interviews stayed limited, but the feedback loop and the ongoing Pixel Perfection workshops moved the design steadily toward something more inclusive and efficient. Design and engineering ended up speaking the same language, and the interface people used every day felt intuitive instead of improvised. That alignment, more than any single screen, is what the project delivered.
